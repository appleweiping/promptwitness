\title{Do Gradients over Reasoning Scale Beyond Tokens?\ An Exact Structural Test of Self-Optimizing Prompts}

\author{Anonymous authors\Paper under double-blind review}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\greater{} showed that a small language model can optimize its own prompt with the
gradient of the answer loss computed over its own reasoning, editing one token at a
time. Prompts, however, are written and reviewed as structured documents, so the
natural next step is to move this feedback from brittle tokens to typed blocks. This
requires scoring edits that change length, delete or insert whole instructions, for
which the one-hot relaxation behind token gradients is undefined. We introduce
\graft{}, an exact structural edit calculus: candidate rewrites of every block share one
sequence in parallel slots behind attention gates, and later tokens move by continuous
slot offsets realized as rotary rotations, so that every vertex is exactly a real
edited prompt. A renormalized one-pass estimator ranks all single-block edits close to
exact evaluation. With exact scores in hand we can ask whether the signal is worth
following. Across three models, four held-out tasks and preregistered replications
with reliability-calibrated targets, \greater{}'s objective ranks structural edits in
the \emph{wrong} direction (pooled Spearman $-0.26$ over eleven runs, including an
untouched task and a third model); the anti-correlation comes from
examples whose reasoning is wrong, where lowering the gold answer's loss rewards
prompts that detach the answer from the reasoning. A preregistered alternative based
on reasoning forks fails replication and a causal test. At the token level the
question cannot be settled by any signal: a single-token edit re-rolls the greedy
reasoning on about a fifth of the questions while moving accuracy by two points.
End to end, \todo{GReaTer gradient vs.\ random shortlist; GRAFT vs.\ baselines}.
Our results locate the gains of gradient-based self-optimization in model-proposed
candidates and fresh-reasoning selection rather than in reasoning-conditioned
gradients, and provide the calculus and protocol to test such signals.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automatic prompt optimization is most attractive where it is hardest: for small,
locally deployed models that cannot rely on a stronger model to critique their
prompts \citep{zhang2024revisiting}. \greater{} \citep{das2025greater} showed that such
models can optimize their own prompts if the optimization signal is a true gradient of
the task loss computed \emph{over the model's own reasoning}: the model reasons under
the current prompt, an extractor elicits its answer, and the gradient of the answer
loss with respect to one-hot prompt tokens shortlists token substitutions, which are
then verified by regenerating the reasoning.

Token coordinates are a mismatch for how prompts are built. Practitioners write
prompts as structured documents---task, strategy, procedure, output contract,
examples---and review them as structural diffs; a token swap cannot delete a harmful
instruction, add a missing procedure or replace a sentence by one of a different
length, and token-level optimization often yields text that is hard to read, such as
the released \greater{} prompt for Llama-3-8B on navigate, ``\emph{Use your reasoning
here. I would like numbers assigned.. to.. To represent moving.}''\footnote{Final-beam
prompts from the official repository's processed logs (commit \texttt{42a22d9}).}
Structure-aware optimizers exist, but they use gradients only to \emph{locate} a
segment \citep{zhao2026gmpo,chakma2026gradpo} or replace gradients by textual feedback
from a strong model \citep{yuksekgonul2024textgrad,sharma2026mpo,agrawal2026gepa}. Can
the gradient signal itself be lifted to blocks?

Two things stand in the way. The first is technical: alternative blocks have different
lengths, so the relaxation into the vocabulary simplex used by \greater{} and GCG
\citep{zou2023gcg} is undefined. The second is conceptual and only becomes testable once
the first is solved: a block edit changes the reasoning it is scored on far more than a
token edit does, so it is unclear whether a gradient computed over \emph{fixed}
reasoning still predicts what the edit does to accuracy. This paper solves the first
problem exactly and then answers the second with preregistered, reliability-calibrated
experiments.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An exact structural edit calculus} (\S\ref{sec:calculus}). All candidate
rewrites of all blocks share one sequence in parallel slots, gated into downstream
attention, with continuous per-slot offsets implemented as extra rotary rotations.
Every vertex---replacement, deletion, insertion and multi-block combinations---is
exactly the corresponding real prompt, verified to fp32 precision on four
architectures.
\item \textbf{A faithful one-pass estimator} (\S\ref{sec:estimator}). First-order gate
derivatives provably saturate and do not rank real edits; renormalized superposed
patching keeps each layer's attention exact and tracks exact evaluation (Spearman
$\approx0.8$).
\item \textbf{A validity test of gradients over reasoning} (\S\ref{sec:validity}). For
LLM-proposed block edits, \greater{}'s objective anti-predicts held-out accuracy; we
trace this to answer--reasoning decoupling on failed examples, and show that a
preregistered decision-token objective does not fix it. Held-out targets are noisy, so
every correlation is reported against a split-half reliability ceiling.
\item \textbf{Token-level non-locality and end-to-end tests} (\S\ref{sec:tokens},
\S\ref{sec:endtoend}). A single-token edit re-rolls about a fifth of greedy answers,
which puts token-level signals below the resolution of standard benchmarks;
\todo{official GReaTer with random vs.\ gradient shortlists; GRAFT vs.\ baselines at
matched cost}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background: gradients over reasoning}
\label{sec:background}

Let $f_\theta$ be a frozen causal language model, $P$ a prompt with input slot, and
$(x,y)$ a task example. \greater{} generates reasoning $r\sim f_\theta(\cdot\mid P(x))$,
appends an extractor $e$ (e.g.\ ``Therefore, the final answer is''), and scores the
answer by
\begin{equation}
\ell(P;x,y,r) = -\log p_\theta\big(y \mid P(x)\oplus r\oplus e\big).
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Holding $r$ fixed, it differentiates $\ell$ with respect to the one-hot indicator
$\epsilon_i$ of prompt token $i$, keeps the $k$ candidates proposed by the model's
own next-token distribution, and ranks them by $\nabla_{\epsilon_i}\ell\cdot(\mathbf e_v-\mathbf e_{p_i})$.
The gradient thus decides which candidates are tried; the official implementation
then regenerates the reasoning under every shortlisted candidate and keeps the one
with the fewest answer errors on the minibatch, plus a small prompt-perplexity
penalty. The vertices of the one-hot simplex are real tokens, which is what makes
the first-order shortlist meaningful. We keep \greater{}'s objective, its
shortlist-then-verify loop and its self-optimization setting, and change the
coordinate from tokens to blocks.

\section{An exact structural edit calculus}
\label{sec:calculus}

\paragraph{Structured prompts.} A prompt is a sequence of typed blocks
$P=(b_1,\dots,b_m)$ inside chat messages; each block has a type (task, strategy,
procedure, output, input, example), an editable flag and required literals, as in
the PromptWitness document model. An empty editable block is an \emph{insertion
slot}. The edit space of slot $j$ is its candidate set $C_j$ (rewrites, or new
blocks for an empty slot) plus deletion.

\paragraph{Superposed sequence.} For one example we build a single token sequence
containing the incumbent prompt $P(x)$, the tokens of every candidate $c\in C_j$
for every slot, and the tail $r\oplus e\oplus y$. Candidate tokens of slot $j$ may
attend only to the prefix before slot $j$ and to earlier tokens of the same
candidate; tokens after slot $j$ may attend to the incumbent and to all candidates
of slot $j$. Attention of query $t$ over its visible keys $u$ is
\begin{equation}
a_{tu}=\frac{G_{tu}\exp(s_{tu}-m_t)}{\sum_{v}G_{tv}\exp(s_{tv}-m_t)},\qquad
G_{tu}=g_{\sigma(u)},
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(u)$ is the segment of key $u$ (an incumbent block or a candidate),
$g$ is a vector of gates and $G_{tu}=1$ for ungated keys and for keys in the
query's own segment. Because the gate multiplies after the max-shift, $g=0$
removes a key exactly and derivatives at $g=0$ are exact one-sided derivatives.

\paragraph{Slot offsets.} Candidates start at their incumbent's first position.
Each slot $j$ carries a continuous offset $\omega_j$; every token $t$ after the
slot is moved by $\Omega_t=\sum_j M_{tj}\omega_j$ positions, where $M_{tj}=1$ iff
$t$ follows slot $j$. Since rotary scores depend only on position differences and
rotations compose, this is an extra rotation $R(\Omega_t)$ of queries and keys:
$s_{tu}=\langle R(\Omega_t)\tilde q_t, R(\Omega_u)\tilde k_u\rangle/\sqrt d$, with
$\tilde q,\tilde k$ the model's rotated vectors.

\begin{proposition}[Exact base point]
At $g^0$ (incumbents $1$, candidates $0$) and $\omega=0$, every prompt and tail
token has the hidden states of $f_\theta$ on $P(x)\oplus r\oplus e\oplus y$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Exact vertices]
Choose for each slot one of \{keep, delete, candidate $c$\}. Set the chosen
candidate's gate to $1$, the incumbent's gate to $0$ if it is replaced or deleted,
and $\omega_j=|c|-|b_j|$ (or $-|b_j|$ for deletion, $|c|$ for insertion). Then the
superposed computation equals $f_\theta$ on the correspondingly edited prompt.
\end{proposition}

Proofs are by induction over layers (Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}): at every layer,
the keys with non-zero gate visible to a non-candidate query are exactly the tokens
preceding it in the edited prompt, their position differences equal those in the
edited prompt, and chosen candidates see exactly their edited-prompt prefix. The
relaxation is therefore the block-level counterpart of the one-hot simplex:
\greater{}'s vertices are real tokens; ours are real structured prompts, of any
length. We verify both propositions numerically in fp32 for Llama, Gemma-2 (with
logit soft-capping) and OLMo-3 architectures, including three simultaneous edits
(Appendix~\ref{app:tests}).

\section{Estimating structural edits in one pass}
\label{sec:estimator}

\paragraph{First-order gate derivatives saturate.} The straight line from the base
point to a replacement vertex gives
$\widehat{\Delta\ell}_{\text{gate}}(b_j\!\to\!c)=\partial_{g_c}\ell-\partial_{g_{b_j}}\ell+(|c|-|b_j|)\,\partial_{\omega_j}\ell$.
For one attention row, with $Z_0$ the existing mass and $S_c=\sum_{u\in c}e^{s_{tu}-m_t}$
the candidate's mass, $\partial o_t/\partial g_c|_{0}=(S_c/Z_0)(\bar v_c-o_t)$ while the
actual insertion moves the output by $\frac{S_c}{Z_0+S_c}(\bar v_c-o_t)$. The linear
extrapolation overshoots by the factor $(Z_0+S_c)/Z_0$, which is unbounded when a
block attracts more mass than the rest of the context---typical for instruction
blocks read by reasoning tokens. Empirically (Appendix~\ref{app:tests}) the estimates
reach hundreds of nats for edits whose true effect is below three, and they do not
rank edits.

\paragraph{Renormalized superposed patching.} The superposed pass already contains,
at every layer $\ell$, the exact keys and values of every candidate. For a
downstream query $t$ of slot $j$ and an alternative $a$ (a candidate or nothing), we
compute the attention output with the slot swapped \emph{exactly},
\begin{equation}
o^{\ell}_t(a)=\frac{N^{\ell}_{\mathrm{pre}}(\delta_a)+N^{\ell}_a(\delta_a)+N^{\ell}_{\mathrm{post}}}
{Z^{\ell}_{\mathrm{pre}}(\delta_a)+Z^{\ell}_a(\delta_a)+Z^{\ell}_{\mathrm{post}}},
\end{equation}
where $N,Z$ are value-weighted and total exponentiated scores over prefix keys,
the alternative's keys and post-slot keys, and $\delta_a$ is the length change,
applied as an exact rotation of the query against prefix and candidate keys.
Only the propagation through later layers is linearized:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\Delta\ell}_{\text{patch}}(a)=\sum_{\ell}\sum_{t\in D_j}
\big\langle \nabla_{o^{\ell}_t}\ell,\; o^{\ell}_t(a)-o^{\ell}_t(b_j)\big\rangle .
\label{eq:patch}
\end{equation}
This carries the key insight of AtP* \citep{kramar2024atp}---keep attention exact,
linearize elsewhere---from interpretability to prompt search; the superposed
layout is what makes every candidate's exact keys and values available in a single
pass. The gradients $\nabla_{o^\ell}\ell$ come from one backward pass, and the extra
attention arithmetic is small next to the forward pass.

\section{Objectives and the shortlist-then-verify loop}
\label{sec:graft}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Structural shortlist-then-verify search (\graft{} and its controls)}
\label{alg:graft}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require model $f_\theta$, typed prompt $P$, train set $D$, rounds $R$, shortlist size $\mu$
\For{$\rho=1,\dots,R$}
  \State sample minibatch $B\subset D$; greedy reasoning $r_i$ under $P$
  \State propose $K$ label-free candidates per slot with $f_\theta$ (unlabeled inputs only)
  \State filter by PromptWitness policies (required literals, variables, clean tokenization)
  \State score every edit on $B$: \graft{} by Eq.~\ref{eq:patch}; controls: exact loss, random, raw gate
  \State regenerate reasoning for the top-$\mu$ edits on $B$
  \State accept the best if it improves minibatch accuracy (ties: answer loss)
\EndFor
\State select among the incumbents of rounds $0,R/3,2R/3,R$ on dev; evaluate once on test
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:graft} lifts \greater{}'s loop to blocks: the model proposes
candidates, a gradient score shortlists them, and fresh reasoning decides. As in
\greater{}, the model optimizes its own prompt and supervision enters only through the
loss and the verification; \greater{} proposes the model's top-$k$ next tokens, we ask
the model for type-conditioned rewrites or new blocks given unlabeled task inputs.
Controls differ \emph{only} in the shortlist: exact fixed-reasoning loss of every edited
prompt (one forward pass per edit and example), a random shortlist, and raw gate
derivatives.

\paragraph{Objectives.} The main objective is \greater{}'s (Eq.~\ref{eq:loss}). We also
test a \emph{decision margin at reasoning forks}. Correct and incorrect reasoning of
the same model on the same question share a prefix and part at few tokens
\citep{bigelow2025forking,lin2024critical,shen2026decision}. We sample $S$ reasonings per
training example and pair the greedy reasoning with every sample of opposite
correctness; their first divergence defines a fork with prefix $r_{<t}$, the token $a^+$
of the correct branch and $a^-$ of the incorrect one, and
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\text{fork}}(P)= z_\theta\big(a^-\mid P(x)\oplus r_{<t}\big)-z_\theta\big(a^+\mid P(x)\oplus r_{<t}\big),
\label{eq:fork}
\end{equation}
averaged over at most three forks per example (the softmax normalizer cancels). Prefix
tokens are downstream queries of every slot, so one superposed pass scores all edits on
all forks. A rationale-likelihood objective in the style of PMPO
\citep{zhao2025pmpo,zelikman2022star} was weaker in a pilot and is reported in the appendix.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and data.} Task and optimizer model coincide, as in \greater{}:
Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-it (\greater{}'s models) and Qwen3-8B in non-thinking
mode. Benchmarks are \greater{}'s 21 BIG-Bench Hard tasks \citep{suzgun2023bbh} (50
train, 100 dev, up to 100 test), GSM8K \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k} (100/100/1{,}319) and FOLIO
\citep{han2024folio} (50/100/203), from the \greater{} release with a fixed-seed split.
41\% of our BBH test rows were among the rows \greater{} used to produce its published
prompts, so comparisons with those prompts are also reported on the clean remainder.
Validity experiments use four BBH tasks outside \greater{}'s 21 (logical deduction and
tracking shuffled objects, three and seven objects) with 200 held-out questions each;
they are never used for benchmarking.

\paragraph{Shared reader.} Every prompt is evaluated identically: greedy reasoning (up to
1{,}024 tokens, \greater{}'s own budget), the task-typed extractor inside the assistant
turn, and 16 greedy answer tokens parsed by answer type, tolerant of Markdown and
\LaTeX{} wrappers. All dev selections, test reads and fixed prompts use one inference
engine with identical token-level rendering (verified to be token-identical to the
training-time renderer).

\paragraph{Preregistration.} Hypotheses, replication protocols and every amendment are
dated in the experiment plan before the data they concern were inspected; reported
negative outcomes (H-fork, \S\ref{sec:forks}) follow that plan.

\section{Do gradients over reasoning predict what structural edits do?}
\label{sec:validity}

\paragraph{Design.} Search can succeed or fail for many reasons, so we test the signal
directly. For a task and an incumbent prompt we draw 24 training rows and let the model
propose label-free rewrites and insertions for every slot (20--26 edits per pool). Each
predictor is computed on the 24 rows: the change in \greater{}'s answer loss, exactly and
by patching; the change in fork margin (Eq.~\ref{eq:fork}), exactly and by patching; and,
as the evaluation-based reference, fresh greedy accuracy of every edited prompt on 8 and
on 24 rows. The target is the change in greedy accuracy on 200 held-out questions.
Replication \emph{v3} uses independent seeds, pools and held-out sets on two models and
three tasks, plus a non-initial prompt state, under a protocol frozen before its data.

\paragraph{The target is noisy.} Two random halves of the held-out questions rank the
same edits with Spearman correlation $\rho_{1/2}$; the Spearman--Brown reliability
$\kappa=2\rho_{1/2}/(1+\rho_{1/2})$ gives a noise ceiling $\sqrt{\kappa}$ for what any
predictor can reach. Differences between predictors are tested with a paired bootstrap
over training rows and held-out questions (conditional on the pools).

\paragraph{\greater{}'s objective anti-predicts block edits.} Table~\ref{tab:validity}
and Figure~\ref{fig:validity}: the exact answer-loss change correlates \emph{negatively}
with the held-out change (pooled over eleven runs $-0.26$ [$-0.27,-0.10$]); patching
tracks the exact values (mean Spearman $0.79$ between them, range $0.53$--$0.97$ over the
eleven pools) and inherits the
sign ($-0.20$). Fresh accuracy on the same 24 rows is positive ($+0.32$ [$+0.14,+0.35$]).
The prospective test on an untouched task with three models (TS3 rows), whose targets
are highly reliable ($\sqrt{\kappa}\ge0.90$), repeats the pattern: the patched
answer-loss shortlist picks top-3 edits worse than a random ranking (best-3 regret
$0.114$ vs.\ $0.057$). The estimator is not the problem; the objective is.

\paragraph{Why: failed reasoning rewards decoupling.} Splitting the training rows by
whether the incumbent's greedy answer was right isolates the effect. On Llama-3 the
answer-loss score computed only on rows answered \emph{wrongly} correlates $-0.61$,
$-0.48$, $-0.29$ and $-0.52$ with the held-out change; on rows answered correctly it is
near zero. After a wrong rationale, the gold answer has low probability exactly when
the answer follows the rationale; an edit lowers that loss by making the answer depend
less on the reasoning. Consistent with this, on the run with the most wrong rows the
edit-level change in the gold answer's loss is almost the mirror image of the change in
the loss of the model's \emph{own} answer (Spearman $-0.91$), and edits that loosen the
model's own answer lower held-out accuracy ($-0.40$). The effect is the optimization
counterpart of unfaithful chain-of-thought \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023faithfulness}.
The obvious repair, scoring only rows the incumbent answers correctly, removes the
harmful part but leaves no useful signal: preregistered before its data, it did not
beat the all-row score on the untouched task and ranked top edits worse than chance
(best-3 regret $-0.057$ relative to random, $P=0.001$).

\paragraph{Decision forks do not rescue the signal.}
\label{sec:forks}
Our preregistered hypothesis H-fork stated that the fork margin predicts held-out change
better than the answer loss and than fresh accuracy on 8 rows, and that forcing the
correct fork token raises the success probability more than forcing a token at a random
position of the shared prefix. It failed: the fork margin is $-0.13$ pooled, below fresh
accuracy on 8 rows (difference $-0.36$, $P(\text{fork}>\text{fresh})=0.017$), and not
reliably above the answer loss ($+0.17$, $P=0.90$). In the causal test (six runs, 190
forks, six continuations per arm), first divergences change the success probability no
more than random positions ($|\Delta P_{\text{fork}}|-|\Delta P_{\text{ctrl}}|=-0.01$
[$-0.04,+0.03$]): the first place where a correct and an incorrect rationale differ is
usually wording, not the decision.

\section{Tokens: one edit re-rolls the reasoning}
\label{sec:tokens}
The block result invites a locality explanation: \greater{}'s edits are single tokens,
so the reasoning they are scored on might barely change. We repeat the validity study
with \greater{}-style edits---single-token substitutions drawn from the model's own
top-10 next tokens at a prompt position---on the same rows and held-out questions.
Locality does not hold: a single-token edit changes the greedy answer on 16--22\% of the
200 held-out questions, about as many as a block edit (6--38\%), while net accuracy moves
by only $\pm2$ points (standard deviation $0.020$--$0.025$ across edits, four runs on two
models). Such changes are what random re-rolling of a fifth of the answers would produce,
and the split-half reliability of the held-out target is $0.05$--$0.26$: at this
granularity no signal, gradient or evaluation, is distinguishable from noise on a
200-question set (pooled Spearman of the answer loss $-0.10$ [$-0.22,+0.11$], of fresh
accuracy $+0.06$ [$-0.14,+0.20$]; Table~\ref{tab:validity-token}), and a single benchmark
split cannot tell good token edits from lucky ones.

\section{End-to-end: where do the gains of self-optimization come from?}
\label{sec:endtoend}

\paragraph{Published \greater{} prompts.} With the shared reader, \greater{}'s released
prompts for Llama-3-8B improve on its initial prompt by $+4.7$ points averaged over 21
BBH tasks ($+3.4$ [$+0.5,+6.4$] on test rows \greater{} never saw), concentrated in a few
tasks (tracking shuffled objects $+32$, geometric shapes $+22$, object counting $+18$).
For Gemma-2-9B the gain is $+3.3$ points on both the full split ($[+1.2,+5.2]$) and
the clean rows ($[+0.6,+6.1]$), winning 13 of 20 tasks. The gains are real; the question
is which part of \greater{} produces them.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Gradient-based discrete prompt search.} AutoPrompt \citep{shin2020autoprompt},
GCG \citep{zou2023gcg}, PEZ \citep{wen2023hard} and \greater{} \citep{das2025greater}
relax tokens into the vocabulary simplex or embedding space; \greater{} takes the
gradient over the model's own reasoning so that small models can optimize themselves,
where LLM-driven optimizers struggle \citep{zhang2024revisiting}. \graft{} relaxes blocks
with exact vertices for length-changing edits, and we test the reasoning-conditioned
signal itself rather than a search pipeline built on it.

\paragraph{Evaluation- and feedback-driven optimization.} APE \citep{zhou2023ape}, OPRO
\citep{yang2024opro}, ProTeGi \citep{pryzant2023protegi}, EvoPrompt \citep{guo2024evoprompt},
TextGrad \citep{yuksekgonul2024textgrad}, MIPRO \citep{opsahl2024mipro} and GEPA
\citep{agrawal2026gepa} propose candidates with an LLM and decide by measured scores or
natural-language critiques. Structure-aware variants edit sections or typed units:
SAMMO \citep{schnabel2024sammo}, SPRIG \citep{zhang2024sprig}, section-local textual
gradients \citep{sharma2026mpo} and SEPO \citep{ma2026sepo}; GMPO, GradPO and PMPO use
gradients or masking to \emph{locate} segments and score rewrites by evaluation or
rationale likelihood \citep{zhao2026gmpo,chakma2026gradpo,zhao2025pmpo}. Our results
explain why evaluation remains necessary for structural edits: the gradient signal over
fixed reasoning is misaligned with what such edits do.

\paragraph{Reliability of the optimization signal.} p1 \citep{gao2026p1} decomposes reward
variance into response noise and prompt-quality variance and selects training prompts
where the latter dominates; HbBoPs \citep{schneider2025hbbops} allocates evaluations
adaptively; \citet{sadjoli2025optimization} show that prompt optimization changes model
rankings. We add a split-half ceiling for \emph{ranking edits} and show that at token
granularity the edit-quality variance is below the resolution of standard splits.

\paragraph{Reasoning faithfulness and decision points.} Chain-of-thought answers can
depart from the stated reasoning \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,lanham2023faithfulness}; we
find that \greater{}'s objective rewards exactly this departure on failed examples.
Sampled reasoning branches at few decisive tokens
\citep{bigelow2025forking,jia2026forking,lin2024critical,abdin2024phi4,shen2026decision};
our causal test shows that the \emph{first} divergence between a correct and an incorrect
rationale is usually not such a token. ContraPrompt \citep{rishav2026contraprompt} and
contrastive reflection \citep{koh2026contrastive} contrast traces in text through a
reflecting LLM.

\paragraph{Attribution and parallel contexts.} Attribution patching and AtP*
\citep{syed2024atp,kramar2024atp} estimate interventions to first order; ContextCite
\citep{cohenwang2024contextcite} attributes generations to context sources; parallel
context windows \citep{ratner2023pcw} and superposition prompting
\citep{merth2024superposition} process segments in parallel paths. We combine parallel
paths, exact positional offsets and exact attention to evaluate structural edits.

\section{Limitations}
Our validity experiments use LLM-proposed edits from one proposal procedure and
multiple-choice reasoning tasks; the anti-correlation may be weaker for other proposal
distributions or answer types, and intervals are conditional on the proposal pools.
Correctness of a rationale is judged by its extracted answer, and the decoupling
mechanism rests on correlational evidence from few runs. The exact calculus needs
white-box access to models with rotary embeddings and softmax attention and edits that
tokenize cleanly at block boundaries; the one-pass estimator is not faster than exact
scoring at the pool sizes we use. Held-out targets of 200 questions are noisy, which we
quantify but cannot remove, and all experiments ran on two shared 48\,GB GPUs, which
bounds seeds and tasks. \todo{Update after end-to-end results.}

\section{Proofs}
\label{app:proofs}

\paragraph{Setting.} We consider decoder-only transformers whose token mixing is
causal softmax attention with rotary position embeddings, possibly with logit
soft-capping and grouped-query attention; all other sublayers act on tokens
independently. We require that edits \emph{tokenize cleanly}: the edited prompt's
token sequence is the incumbent's prefix tokens, the candidate's tokens and the
incumbent's suffix tokens (edits violating this are excluded, which happens rarely
because blocks end at line breaks). Sliding windows are assumed to exceed the
sequence length, which our implementation asserts.

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition 1.} By induction over layers we show that every
non-candidate token $t$ has the hidden state of the reference computation on
$P(x)\oplus r\oplus e\oplus y$. At the input, token identities and positions agree
($\Omega=0$). At a layer, the keys visible to $t$ are the non-candidate tokens that
precede it in the reference order, with gate $1$, and candidate tokens with gate
$0$. The latter contribute $0\cdot\exp(s_{tu}-m_t)=0$ to both the numerator and the
denominator of Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}; the max-shift $m_t$ may include them but softmax
is shift-invariant. Keys and values of the former equal the reference ones by the
induction hypothesis, and all other sublayers are tokenwise. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Proof of Proposition 2.} Fix a choice $e_j\in\{\text{keep},\text{delete},c_j\}$
per slot and let $P'$ be the edited prompt. Map every retained non-candidate token
$t$ and every chosen candidate token to its counterpart in $P'$. Its position in
$P'$ is its base position plus the cumulative length change of the edits before
it, $\sum_{k\prec t}(|e_k|-|b_k|)$, which is exactly $\pi_t+\Omega_t$ at
$\omega_k=|e_k|-|b_k|$ (candidates start at their slot's first position and move
only with earlier slots). The keys with non-zero gate visible to a mapped query are
exactly the tokens preceding it in $P'$: removed incumbents and unchosen
candidates have gate $0$; a chosen candidate of slot $j$ sees the prefix before
slot $j$ and the chosen candidates of earlier slots; a later token sees retained
incumbents and chosen candidates. Rotary scores depend only on position
differences, which agree with $P'$. The induction of Proposition~1 then applies
verbatim to the mapped tokens, so the loss equals $\ell(P';x,y,r)$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Saturation of gate derivatives.} For one attention row, write the
current output as $o_0=N_0/Z_0$ with $N_0=\sum_{u\notin c}G_{tu}e^{s_{tu}-m}v_u$,
$Z_0=\sum_{u\notin c}G_{tu}e^{s_{tu}-m}$, and let $S_c=\sum_{u\in c}e^{s_{tu}-m}$,
$\bar v_c=S_c^{-1}\sum_{u\in c}e^{s_{tu}-m}v_u$. Then
$o(g)=\frac{N_0+gS_c\bar v_c}{Z_0+gS_c}$, so
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\partial o}{\partial g}\Big|_{0}=\frac{S_c}{Z_0}\,(\bar v_c-o_0),\qquad
o(1)-o(0)=\frac{S_c}{Z_0+S_c}\,(\bar v_c-o_0).
\end{equation*}
The first-order extrapolation exceeds the actual change by the factor
$1+S_c/Z_0$ in every head and layer, before any downstream nonlinearity. A
candidate block that competes with an instruction block already holding most of a
reading token's attention has $S_c/Z_0\gg1$, so raw gate derivatives are dominated
by attention mass rather than by the effect of the edit. The renormalized estimator
of Eq.~\ref{eq:patch} uses $o(1)-o(0)$ exactly.

\section{Numerical verification}
\label{app:tests}
All exactness claims are checked in fp32 on small randomly initialized models of four
architectures (Llama, Gemma-2 with attention and final logit soft-capping, OLMo-3 and
Qwen3), using the real Llama-3 tokenizer and chat template. For the base point, every
replacement and deletion vertex, insertion into an empty slot, and a combined vertex with
three simultaneous edits of different lengths, the superposed loss equals the loss of the
stock model on the real edited prompt to within $10^{-4}$ nats. Gate and offset gradients
agree with central finite differences to within $2\cdot10^{-3}+2\%$. The weighted
rationale objective and the decision margin (Eq.~\ref{eq:fork}) match stock computations
to $10^{-4}$, and renormalized patching reconstructs the base computation to $10^{-4}$.
A control confirms that the approximate right-padded and left-padded layouts are
\emph{not} exact, so the tests detect the offset construction. The test suite
(15 tests) is part of the released code.

\section{Experimental details}
\label{app:details}

\paragraph{Prompts and proposals.} The initial prompt splits \greater{}'s instruction into
a strategy block (``Use proper logical reasoning and think step by step.''), an empty
procedure slot and an output block (``Finally give the actual correct answer.''). The
task model proposes candidates from two unlabeled training questions with a fixed
meta-prompt per operator (rewrite: rephrase, specialize, add a step, shorten, warn about a
pitfall, number the procedure, clarify the final answer; insert: procedure, verification,
organization of facts, common mistake, comparison of options, restating facts, strategy
hint, double-checking), sampled at temperature 0.8. Proposals are cleaned of preambles and
type labels and must tokenize cleanly at block boundaries.

\paragraph{Reader.} Greedy reasoning up to 1{,}024 tokens (384 in the validity studies,
whose tasks need fewer), stopped at the chat template's end-of-turn tokens; the extractor
is appended inside the assistant turn (``Therefore, the final answer is ('' for
multiple choice; ``(Yes or No) is:'' style for binary tasks; ``(a single integer) is:''
for counting and arithmetic); 16 greedy answer tokens are parsed by answer type, tolerant
of Markdown emphasis and \LaTeX{} wrappers such as \verb|\boxed{}|. Qwen3 runs in
non-thinking mode. Rendered token ids are identical across the training-time and
evaluation engines for all three models.

\paragraph{Validity studies.} Per run: 2 training questions for proposals, 24 training
rows for predictors, 6 sampled rationales per row (temperature 0.7) for forks, at most 3
forks per row, and 200 held-out questions for the target; seeds 101 (v3), 202 with a
non-initial state (v3), 303 (TS3). The paired bootstrap resamples training rows and
held-out questions jointly for all predictors (2{,}000 replicates) and keeps the proposal
pools fixed, so intervals are conditional on the pools.

\paragraph{Causal fork test.} For 32 training rows per run, forks are computed as in the
validity study. For each fork we force the correct-branch and the incorrect-branch token
after the shared prefix and sample six continuations each; the control forces, at a
uniformly random position of the shared prefix, the actual token versus the model's most
likely different token.
