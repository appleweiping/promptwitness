\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automatic prompt optimization is most useful for small, locally deployed models that cannot rely on
a stronger model to critique their prompts \citep{zhang2024revisiting}. \greater{}
\citep{das2025greater} lets such a model optimize its own prompt: the gradient of the answer loss
with respect to one-hot prompt tokens, taken over the model's reasoning generated once and then
held \emph{fixed}, shortlists token substitutions, and regenerated reasoning decides which one is
kept.

This gradient measures how an edit changes the answer read off a given reasoning, not how it
changes the reasoning. Yet prompts are structured documents (task, strategy, procedure, output
contract), revised by rewriting, deleting or inserting whole instructions precisely to change how
the model reasons. Does a gradient over fixed reasoning predict what such edits do? We make
\greater{}'s fixed-reasoning objective exact for block edits of any length, so that a failure of the
signal cannot be a failure of a relaxation (\S\ref{sec:calculus}), and test it on eleven pools of
block edits that Llama-3-8B, Qwen3-8B and Gemma-2-9B propose for their own prompts, collected under
a protocol fixed before their data (the hypotheses registered for these pools, a fork margin and an
answer-conditioned repair, both failed).

\textbf{The signal points the wrong way} (\S\ref{sec:diagnosis}). The fixed-reasoning objective
correlates negatively with the held-out accuracy change in $8$ of $11$ pools (pooled Spearman
$-0.26$, sign-flip $p=0.017$), while fresh greedy accuracy on $8$ of the same training questions is
positive in $9$ ($+0.28$). Even a single-token substitution changes the greedy reasoning on
$79$--$93\%$ of questions (numerical re-rolls of the unchanged prompt: $58$--$85\%$); its mean
accuracy does not differ detectably from that of the re-rolls (permutation $p=0.07$--$0.88$;
exploratory), although edits flip correctness on more questions in three of four pools.

\textbf{Edits act on the reasoning, which the signal does not see} (\S\ref{sec:theory}). An edit's
effect decomposes exactly into a distribution term, carried by the reasoning the model writes, and a
read-off term, of which the objective is a soft version on one reasoning. On the models' own
samples the read-off term averages at most $1.2$ accuracy points per edit against mean absolute
held-out effects of $2.1$--$10.9$.

\textbf{The principled repair fails its preregistered test} (\S\ref{sec:exp-validity}). Reweighting
the incumbent's samples by exact likelihood ratios reaches a pooled Spearman of only $+0.03$ and a
best-3 regret above a random shortlist's, as coverage predicts: importance sampling needs about
$e^{\mathrm{KL}}$ samples \citep{chatterjee2018sample}; in $10$ of $11$ pools the median edit
moves the distribution by more than $\log 16$ nats (in the direction the incumbent's samples can
measure), and without tempering the median effective sample size is $1.4$--$5.1$ of $16$.

\textbf{Verification decides} (\S\ref{sec:exp-verify}; exploratory). On fresh reasoning, ranking
quality rises monotonically with the generations per candidate. Accepting the best candidate on
$16$ or fewer generations lowers held-out accuracy, a winner's curse. In a replay at a fixed budget
of candidate reads, adaptive allocation reduces this loss without removing it, whereas accepting only
gains above two standard errors cuts the loss of uniform verification at up to $12$ questions per
candidate from $1.1$--$1.9$ to $0.2$--$0.6$ points. In these single-round replays, verification and
a calibrated acceptance rule pay off, and the fixed-reasoning shortlist does no better than a random
